Chapter One: Before the Silence

The house on the hill did not look like a place where sorrow could ever find room to settle.

It stood broad and proud at the edge of Kampala's quieter side, where the roads were paved smooth and the trees were trimmed with intention and the neighbours greeted each other by name in the evenings when the heat had begun to soften. It was the kind of house that made children feel safe simply by existing — wide-verandaed, with its compound large enough for a boy to run until his lungs gave out, its sitting room full of furniture that had been chosen carefully, its kitchen always warm with something on the stove. But what made the house what it truly was had nothing to do with the walls or the compound or the furniture. It had everything to do with the people inside it.

Wanyakala had built that life from ambition and patience, the two things he possessed in equal and extraordinary measure. He was not a man who spoke often about where he had come from, but those who knew him well enough understood that everything around him — the company in the city that bore his name in small gold letters on the front door, the cars parked in the compound, the school fees paid without flinching — had been assembled piece by piece over years of quiet, disciplined work. He was tall and measured in his words, and he carried himself with the particular calm of a man who has learned that the world respects those who do not hurry. In meetings, other men leaned forward when he finally chose to speak. On the street, strangers moved aside without quite knowing why.

But at home, in the presence of his wife, something in him loosened completely.

He laughed more at home. He lingered at the dinner table longer than necessary, refilling his cup and starting conversations that had no agenda. He watched football on Sunday evenings with his shoes off and his feet on the coffee table in the way his own mother had told him was undignified, and he did it without shame because Natunga had never once made him feel that ease was something he had to earn. He became, in the most private and essential sense, simply a husband. A man loved well enough to be comfortable in his own skin.

And Natunga made that possible.

She was the kind of woman whose presence rearranged the temperature of a room — not through noise or performance, but through the particular quality of warmth that some people carry so naturally it feels like climate. She moved through each day with both hands open and an unhurried heart, as though she had privately understood something the rest of the world was still working toward: that the ordinary moments are the ones worth protecting, that the meal cooked on a Tuesday evening matters as much as the grand occasions, that love is most truly expressed in the daily, unglamorous act of showing up.

She kept the house not as a duty but as a form of devotion. The meals she cooked carried the flavour of intention — she chose her spices with care, tasted as she went, and always called the family to the table before the food had a chance to lose its best heat. She knew which chair her husband preferred, which side of the bed her son migrated to in the night, which songs made Otim the house boy hum to himself in the kitchen. She kept track of the world around her with a quiet attentiveness that those who benefited from it often mistook for effortlessness.

She was not effortless. She was disciplined in her love, and that made all the difference.

The way she called her son's name carried the full weight of that love.

Mooli.

He was four years old, and the world had not yet shown him its other face.

He was a small boy with large, watchful eyes that seemed always to be recording something — the way morning light entered the sitting room at an angle and landed on the far wall in a shape that changed as the minutes passed, the sound of his mother's slippers on the kitchen tiles in the early morning before anyone else was awake, the particular rhythm of his father's knock at the compound gate each evening that was somehow different from any other knock in the world and therefore unmistakable from fifty metres away.

He had been enrolled in one of Kampala's most expensive schools — the kind where the classrooms smelled of new textbooks and the teachers spoke careful, deliberate English and the children arrived each morning in pressed uniforms that their mothers had ironed with something close to ceremony. The fees for such a school were not small. Wanyakala paid them without discussion, because this was the kind of father he was — one who had decided early what his child deserved and arranged his life accordingly.

Mooli was not yet old enough to understand what the school represented or to feel the weight of his father's sacrifice. What he understood was simpler and more complete: the going and the returning, the lunch his mother packed with a small folded note inside that he could not yet read but that he carried in his pocket all day because it smelled of her hands. The face waiting at the gate at the end of every afternoon.

That face was always Natunga's.

Life inside that house was not perfect in the way of stories — no life is — but it was whole. It had the integrity of something built with care rather than speed, tended rather than displayed. Wanyakala worked and came home. Natunga cooked and sang in the kitchen and prayed in the early mornings before anyone else was awake, her voice low and private, directed upward with the confidence of someone who has established a reliable relationship with the divine. Mooli played and ate and slept and woke again into the same warm world, so consistently and so completely that the warmth became invisible to him the way that walls become invisible to those who have always lived inside them.

There was Otim too, the house boy, who had come to them when Natunga was still carrying Mooli and had stayed because the household felt worth staying in. He was a young man from the north who moved through the compound with the quiet efficiency of someone who understood his role and took pride in it. He swept the veranda each morning before the family rose. He polished the cars on Saturdays. He kept track of the kitchen supplies without being asked and reported any shortages to Natunga with the seriousness of someone delivering intelligence. He had, over the years, become less an employee and more a kind of fixture — reliable in the way that certain people become part of a family's architecture without anyone consciously deciding it.

Mooli trusted Otim completely, in the way children trust those who are consistently present and consistently kind.

Those were the years that Mooli would later struggle to fully describe — not because they were complicated, but because happiness, when it is complete, does not announce itself. It simply is. The mornings rose without drama. The evenings folded gently into night. The house breathed and the family breathed with it, all of them unaware, as people always are in the good times, that they were living inside something fragile and precious and absolutely impossible to restore once broken.

He would reach for those memories later, in the years when he had nothing. He would find them both sustaining and unbearable — the way a warm coat feels cruelest when you are standing in the cold with nowhere to wear it.

But all of that was still far ahead.

For now, there was only the house on the hill, and the evening smell of Natunga's cooking drifting out through the veranda, and Wanyakala's car turning through the compound gate as the sun went down behind the city, and Mooli running — always running — toward the sound of his father's door.

* * *

Then, without warning or invitation, Natunga began to change.

It did not happen the way illness announces itself in the dramatic stories — no sudden collapse, no sharp cry in the night that brings the whole household running. It crept in the way that bad news often travels: quietly, wearing the disguise of something smaller. She began sleeping longer. She rose later than usual, and when she rose, there was a heaviness in her movements that had not been there before, as though the morning itself had become something she had to push through rather than enter.

She grew tired easily.

A trip to the market that had once taken an hour began taking the whole morning, and she came back from it needing to sit. Cooking, which had always been done with music playing and commentary running and her voice calling questions through to the sitting room, became quieter. The food was still good — she would not let the food suffer — but the joy around its making had dimmed in a way that Wanyakala noticed and did not yet know how to name.

He noticed. He said little at first. He was a man who had learned to be careful with his fears, to keep them contained until he had something more solid than a feeling to work with. He told himself she was tired. He told himself it was the heat, the season, the accumulated business of running a household. He told himself many things, because the alternative — the thing that lived underneath all those explanations — was too large to look at directly.

Mooli noticed too, in the particular way that children notice things: not through understanding but through the body.

He began waking earlier than usual and lying still in the morning dark, listening for her footsteps. He began hovering at the kitchen door during meals, watching her from a distance he could not have explained. He reached for her hand more often — on the walk to the car, at the dinner table, when they sat together in the sitting room in the evenings — and he held it with a grip slightly too firm for simple affection, as though some instinct in him had registered an alarm that his four-year-old mind had no language for yet.

The hospital visits began.

Wanyakala drove her himself, always, in the early mornings before Mooli was awake. They left in the grey light and returned when the day had already started without them. Mooli would come downstairs and find Otim at the stove, the house running on its usual rhythms but emptier, quieter, with a quality of waiting that he felt in his chest without knowing what he was waiting for.

The compound grew quieter. The veranda felt wider. The evenings, which had always had the comfortable density of a family doing ordinary things in the same space, began to feel thinner — like a room from which furniture has been quietly, gradually removed.

Natunga spent more time in the bedroom.

Wanyakala moved their lives carefully around this new reality, saying little to Mooli that was specific and much that was general — she is resting, she needs quiet, she will be better soon. He said these things with the conviction of a man who is praying they are true, and Mooli received them with the trust of a child who has not yet learned that adults sometimes speak their hopes in the tone of facts.

She still smiled when she saw him. That, above everything else, never changed.

Even in the weeks when the bed had become her primary world, when the medicines on the bedside table had multiplied and the doctors came to the house now rather than the other way around — even then, when Mooli appeared in the doorway of her room in the afternoon after school, her face changed. The tiredness was still there, always, a permanent resident behind her eyes. But the smile arrived ahead of it, immediately and completely, and it was the same smile it had always been. It carried the same specific warmth that no other person in the world has ever been able to reproduce for him.

"Mooli," she would say, and open her arms.

He would cross the room and climb in beside her, careful now in a way he had not needed to be before, and he would press his face into her neck and breathe her in. And for those minutes — ten minutes, twenty, however long the afternoon allowed — the house would feel like itself again. Complete and enclosed and warm.

But moments do not hold.

The illness did not have the decency to hurry. It took its time with a thoroughness that felt, to those watching, almost deliberate — as though it wanted Natunga to understand, piece by piece, exactly what was being taken. The kitchen first, then the veranda, then the compound, then the school gate. Each loss came slowly, wrapped in the reassuring language of rest and recovery, until the circle of her world had contracted to the bed, and the window beside it through which she watched the compound she could no longer walk to, and the small boy who came every afternoon and curled against her and refused, absolutely, to understand that this was something that could not be reversed.

Wanyakala sat at her bedside in the evenings and held her hand in both of his. He had stopped talking about work. He had, in some essential way, stopped inhabiting the public version of himself entirely. Outside, the city continued without them — the traffic, the markets, the relentless noise of a place that does not pause for private grief. Inside the house on the hill, time had slowed to the rhythm of medicine schedules and laboured breathing and the particular quality of love that has no way to express itself except through presence.

Mooli did not understand what dying was.

He understood that his mother was tired. He understood that the house was quieter than it used to be. He understood that his father's face, when it thought no one was looking, wore an expression he had never seen on it before and that he did not have a word for — something that looked like a man standing at the edge of something very dark, peering down, trying to find the bottom.

And then one morning, without ceremony, without any final dramatic moment that a child could later point to and say: there, that was it, that was the last time — Natunga was gone.

She was still in the bed when they called Wanyakala in. Her body was still there, composed and still in the way that sleep resembles and yet is nothing at all like. But the thing that had made her Natunga — the specific warmth, the voice that said his name, the eyes that found Mooli first in any room — that had departed sometime in the night, quietly, without asking anyone's permission, without leaving a note.

Mooli was told in the way that adults tell children terrible things: carefully, gently, in words chosen to soften what cannot be softened. Wanyakala held him by the shoulders and looked at him and spoke. The faces of the adults around the room arranged themselves into the postures of grief.

Mooli looked at his father. He looked at the faces. He looked at the door behind which his mother lay no longer breathing.

And then he cried.

He cried the way only the very young can cry — with the whole body, without shame, without the self-consciousness that age eventually installs in all of us. He cried without strategy or performance because he was four years old and he did not yet know that the world would one day expect something more measured. He cried until his body had nothing left to give, and then he sat in his father's arms in the big house on the hill and listened to the silence.

It was not the ordinary silence of a quiet afternoon. It was something else entirely.

It had weight. It had texture. It moved through the rooms and settled into the corners and sat at the dinner table in the chair she had always occupied and stood at the kitchen stove and followed Mooli from room to room with the patience of something that knows it has nowhere else to be.

He was four years old.

And the world, at last, had shown him its other face.

Chapter Two: A House Without Her

Grief, in a home where a mother has died, does not arrive all at once.

It comes in pieces, at unexpected hours, through doors you did not think to lock. It comes the first morning when Mooli wakes and lies still in the early dark, listening for the sound of slippers on kitchen tiles and hearing nothing — just the compound outside, and the distant noise of the city beginning its day, indifferent to what has been lost. It comes at breakfast, when Otim places a plate in front of him with careful hands but without the particular tenderness that transforms food into love. It comes at the school gate, when every other child is collected by a mother and Mooli stands a small distance apart, watching the reunions with something new and uncomfortable in his chest — the first faint awareness of what it means to be without something that others take completely for granted.

It comes, most heavily, in the evenings.

Wanyakala had always been a man of routine. Before Natunga died, the evenings had a shape — a reliable architecture that everyone in the house had moved inside without thinking about it, the way you move inside a well-built structure and do not think about the walls holding it up until the moment something shifts. Dinner at a predictable hour. The television kept to a low, comfortable volume. The sound of his wife moving between rooms, a presence so consistent it had become indistinguishable from the house itself. The boy bathed and in bed and asleep before the city grew too loud and too late.

That shape had been Natunga's creation, held in place by her presence the way a frame holds a painting. Without her, it collapsed almost immediately — not dramatically, not all at once, but in the gradual way of a structure that has lost its central support. Things that had been regular became irregular. Things that had been warm became functional. Things that had been done with love were now done out of habit, and then out of duty, and then, in some cases, not done at all.

Wanyakala began coming home late.

Not out of negligence — those who knew him even slightly understood that. He was not a careless man. He was a man who was drowning in silence, and the office was noise, and noise was the only barrier he had found between himself and the full, unmediated weight of what had happened to his life. He buried himself in the company the way a person buries themselves in any available task when the alternative is sitting still with an unbearable thing: completely, with both hands, taking on more than was necessary and welcoming the exhaustion that came from it because exhaustion at least was a feeling that made sense.

He signed papers until his hand ached. He attended meetings he would previously have delegated. He took calls in the car, in the parking lot, on the drive home through roads that grew darker each evening, so that by the time he turned into the compound, it was late enough that Otim had already seen to Mooli — fed him, bathed him, put him to bed — and the house received Wanyakala in the particular silence of a place where a child is sleeping and an adult has come home too late to matter.

He would stand sometimes at the door of Mooli's room.

Just stand there, in the dark of the corridor, looking in at the small form under the covers, at the rise and fall of the boy's chest — steady, unconscious, trusting the world in the way that children trust it when they are asleep and have not yet learned all the reasons they should not. Something in Wanyakala tightened and released and tightened again at the sight of his son sleeping. He loved the boy with a completeness that had no adequate language. He simply did not know, in those early and difficult months, how to be both a father and a man quietly falling apart. And so he divided himself in the only way he could: present in the ways that felt manageable — the school fees, the house, the compound, Otim kept on and kept paid — and absent in the ways that cost him most, that required him to sit still and feel what he was feeling and be a person that a child could come to with the unformed needs of early grief.

Mooli felt it without having the words for it.

He was four years old, and the vocabulary of abandonment had not yet arrived in him. But the body knows before the mind does. It registers what the mind cannot yet name. He began waking in the night and lying very still, listening to the house breathing around him. He began eating more slowly at meals, prolonging the only part of the day when another human being was reliably present and paying him attention. He began following Otim from room to room with a quietness that the house boy found both endearing and quietly heartbreaking — a small shadow trailing him from kitchen to compound to sitting room, not asking for anything specific, needing simply the fact of company, the evidence that the world still contained people who stayed.

Otim gave him what he could. He was a young man from the north, not trained in grief and not equipped, by temperament or experience, for the kind of sustained emotional presence that the boy needed. But he was consistent and he was kind and he was there, which counted for more than he knew.

* * *

School, which had once been a place of simple and predictable routine, began to complicate itself in ways that Mooli had no framework for.

Children are not born cruel. That is the thing that adults who study such matters always find themselves returning to: cruelty is learned, assembled, accumulated through small permissions and the gradual discovery that making another person smaller is one available route to feeling larger. At four and five years old, in the compound of an expensive Kampala school, the cruelty that Mooli encountered had not yet refined itself into anything sophisticated. But it did not need to be sophisticated to do damage. It only needed to be consistent.

They found him during the lunch breaks, primarily — the older boys, a year or two further along in the system, moving through the school with the particular confidence of children who have never been given reason to doubt their own rightness. They had discovered, with the unerring instinct of those who feed on vulnerability, that Mooli flinched easily. That he went very still when approached by a group. That he looked toward the gate when things became difficult, the way animals look toward exits, and that whoever was supposed to appear at that gate was not coming.

That was enough.

They came back the next day. And the day after that. With the reliability of a bad habit, they found him.

They took his lunch once — pulled the container from his hands and passed it between themselves, laughing at something private that Mooli was not part of. They scattered his books another afternoon, kicking them across the dusty ground and watching him scramble to retrieve them, the humiliation of the scramble being the point, the books themselves irrelevant. They gave him a name — something designed to follow him from the classroom to the playground to the inside of his own head, where names like that do their worst and most lasting work.

Mooli said nothing to Wanyakala.

This was not an accident. Even at four and five, he understood, with the particular intelligence of a child who is paying close attention to the adult world, that his father was carrying something enormous and fragile, something that a wrong word or a new problem might shatter entirely. He had seen his father's face in the car on the way to school — the distance in it, the quality of a man present in body only, his mind living somewhere further back and further down. He had seen how Wanyakala held himself at the dinner table in the evenings: carefully, as a person holds themselves when they are afraid that stillness is the only thing preventing collapse.

And so Mooli carried the school and the bullies and the empty compound evenings and the chair at the dinner table where his mother used to sit — he carried all of it by himself, in the private, costly way that children carry things when they love their parents too much to add to their weight.

He was learning, without anyone intending to teach him, that the world outside the house on the hill was not the same world his mother had helped to build inside it. Inside, there had been warmth and protection and the sense that he was known. Outside — at school, in the spaces between the school gate and the car, in the moments when no adult eye was on him — the world was more indifferent than he had previously understood. It did not care that he had lost something. It did not adjust itself to his grief. It continued on its own terms and expected him to continue on them too.

He was four years old, and five, and the world was teaching him to be harder than he wanted to be.

* * *

Wanyakala, for all his absence, was not a man without feeling.

He noticed the changes in his son with the peripheral awareness of a father whose guilt makes him sharper than he might otherwise be. He noticed the quietness that had replaced the running — Mooli had always run through the compound before, at a speed that suggested the compound was too small for the energy inside him, and now he moved through it slowly, at the pace of someone who has stopped expecting to arrive anywhere good. He noticed how the boy reached for his hand at the dinner table and held it too firmly. He noticed how Mooli's eyes tracked him in the mornings as he prepared to leave for the office, following him to the door and then to the gate, the small face at the window as the car pulled out — watching, always watching.

He noticed and he felt the sharp, familiar edge of his own inadequacy and he did not know what to do with it.

He was a man who knew how to solve problems. He had built a company from the foundation up. He had navigated the complex politics of business in a city where the rules changed depending on who was in the room. He had always been, in the professional realm, capable and decisive and equal to what was asked of him.

His son's grief was a problem he did not know how to solve. And so he did what inadequate people do with problems they cannot solve: he worked longer hours, and told himself it was necessary, and came home to find the boy already asleep.

It was Matthew who finally said what needed to be said.

Matthew was not the kind of friend who appeared often in Wanyakala's life — too blunt for that, too little interested in the softening that social relationships usually require, too willing to say the uncomfortable thing directly and without the wrapping of diplomatic language. They had known each other since before the company existed, since the years when Wanyakala's ambition outweighed his means by a considerable distance and Matthew had sat with him over cheap tea in a place that no longer exists and told him, with a matter-of-factness that left no room for doubt: you are going to build something. I have seen enough men fail to know what not failing looks like, and you are not going to fail.

He had been right. And Wanyakala had never forgotten it.

He was trusted, therefore, in the way that very few people are trusted: completely, without condition, even when — especially when — what he said was difficult.

He came to the office on a Tuesday afternoon, unannounced in the way that old friends feel entitled to arrive, and settled himself in the chair across from Wanyakala's desk with the ease of someone who has sat in that chair many times and intends to sit in it many more. He looked at Wanyakala for a long moment without speaking — at the tiredness that had taken up permanent residence around his eyes, at the looseness in his shoulders that had not been there before, at the particular quality of a man who has stopped expecting the day to offer him anything.

"You are disappearing," Matthew said.

Wanyakala looked at his hands on the desk. He said nothing. Which was, as Matthew understood it, its own kind of agreement.

"She is gone," Matthew said, and his voice carried the gentleness of a man who knows that truth, delivered without love, is simply cruelty in better clothes. "She is gone and she is not coming back. And I know that. And you know that. And the boy at home —" he paused, "the boy at home needs a father who comes back."

Still Wanyakala said nothing.

"Not just the fees," Matthew continued. "Not just the school and the compound and Otim keeping everything running. Present. Actually present. The boy needs you present."

Wanyakala turned his face toward the window. Outside, Kampala moved on its own timetable — the traffic, the noise, the eternal indifference of a city that does not slow down for private losses. He watched it for a moment, this city he had arrived in with nothing and built something in, and he felt the size of what had been taken from him and did not try to reduce it or manage it or put it away.

"You need a family again," Matthew said.

The word hung between them.

Not to erase her. Not to replace what could not be replaced. But a house needs life in it. A boy needs a mother's presence. And a man like Wanyakala — Matthew knew him well enough to say this — was not built for solitude. He was built for a household, for the noise and weight of people who needed him, for the specific kind of purpose that comes from being the center of something larger than yourself.

He did not argue with Matthew.

He drove home that evening earlier than he had in months — early enough to arrive while the light was still in the compound, early enough to hear the compound sounds and smell dinner not quite finished, early enough to matter.

Otim looked up from the kitchen with an expression that was carefully neutral but could not quite conceal its relief.

Mooli was in the sitting room, cross-legged on the floor with a picture book open across his lap. He looked up when his father came through the door — and for a moment, just a moment, his face did something that Wanyakala had not seen it do in months. Something opened in it. Something that had been braced, that had been holding itself carefully in preparation for another evening of not enough, released. The boy did not run to him or cry out or perform the reunion in any dramatic way. He simply looked at his father, and the looking said everything.

Wanyakala sat down on the floor beside his son.

They did not speak very much. Wanyakala was not yet a man who had found the language for what had happened to them, and Mooli was not yet old enough to need language when presence would do. They sat together on the sitting room floor of the big house on the hill, father and son, the city going on outside without them. The picture book lay open between them, unread.

For the first time since Natunga died, the silence felt less like a wound and more like something that could, with time and effort and a small amount of courage, be filled.

Matthew's words stayed with Wanyakala through the evening, through the night, into the morning that followed. He turned them over slowly and carefully, the way you turn over something that has been offered to you and that you are not certain you have the right to accept.

A family again.

He was not yet sure what that meant. He was not sure what it would cost, or what it would ask of him, or whether he had any of it left to give. But the boy on the floor beside him, with his watchful eyes and his careful grip and his four-year-old body braced against a world that had already asked too much of it — that boy was reason enough to try.

In the morning, cautiously, and with the particular kind of hope that has been through grief and come out the other side smaller but more honest, Wanyakala began to consider the future.

Chapter Three: The Woman on the Street

Kampala does not slow down for grief.

It is a city that has learned — through decades of turbulence, through the unrelenting press of population and commerce and the heat that settles over the hills each afternoon like a second sky — to keep moving regardless of what any individual within it is carrying. The matatus pack the roundabouts at rush hour. The traders lay out their goods at the same corners every morning. The city breathes in its particular way — loudly, without pause, with an energy that is equal parts vitality and indifference — and it does not adjust itself for a man whose wife has died and who is now driving through it with one hand on the wheel and his mind somewhere else entirely.

Wanyakala had always loved Kampala. He had arrived in it as a young man with ambition and very little else, and the city had not welcomed him — it never welcomes anyone — but it had allowed him to build inside it, which is the most a city like Kampala ever offers, and he had always considered that enough. He knew its rhythms. He knew which roads filled at which hours, which areas to avoid in the rain, where the best tea could be found at seven in the morning before the proper day began. The city was as familiar to him as the inside of his own house.

But now, driving through it in the weeks after Matthew's visit, he saw it differently. He was looking at it with the eyes of a man who has been reminded that the future is not optional — that it continues whether you participate in it or not, and that the choice, ultimately, is only between the future you build and the one that happens to you while you are busy standing still.

Matthew had planted a seed. Wanyakala had not wanted to receive it. He had sat with it for days, turning it over, setting it aside, picking it up again. He had felt the guilt that comes to men who consider moving forward — as though forward motion were a form of erasure, as though loving again were the same as forgetting. He had sat at Mooli's bedside one evening and watched his son sleep and thought about what it means to be a father, what it actually requires, beyond the fees and the compound and the food on the table, and the answer had arrived in him quietly and without drama: it requires presence. It requires a home that is alive. It requires something that he, alone and retreating further into the office every week, was failing to provide.

He did not stop loving Natunga. He would never stop. That love had changed shape — it had become something that lived in the past tense, something he carried rather than something he moved through — but it was not gone and it was not going. He understood, finally, that moving forward was not betrayal. It was survival. And it was, more importantly, what his son needed from him.

He began, tentatively, to rejoin the world.

He started coming home at normal hours. He resumed a social life that had quietly suspended itself after the funeral — dinners with associates, the occasional evening out with Matthew and Matthew's wife, the small re-engagements with the ordinary texture of life that grief had stripped away. He allowed himself to notice, again, what was around him. He allowed his eyes to move through the world with something other than the fixed inward gaze of a man in mourning.

And so it was that on an ordinary Saturday afternoon in Kampala — the kind of afternoon where the light comes slanted and golden through the trees along the road and the city hums at a lower register than usual — Wanyakala drove through a street he had driven through a hundred times and saw a woman he had never seen before.

She was standing at the edge of the pavement.

The wanting becomes calculation. The warmth becomes strategy. The commitment becomes something that depends, more and more, on whether the thing being committed to is delivering what was expected.

Mariana had expected certain things from her marriage to Wanyakala. She had expected, and received, comfort — the house on the hill, the cars in the compound, the school that Mirembe attended, the position in society that comes with being the wife of a successful man in a city that pays close attention to such things. She had expected, and received, a husband who was present and responsible and not unkind. She had expected a family, and she had one.

What she had not expected — what she had perhaps not properly understood when she accepted the lift on that Saturday afternoon and gave Wanyakala her number — was Mooli.

Not Mooli as a small boy who could be managed and shaped and gradually incorporated. But Mooli as he was becoming: a child who saw things, who remembered things, who carried in those large watchful eyes an intelligence that did not miss much and forgot even less. A child who had, somewhere in the quiet interior of himself, never fully surrendered his guardedness toward her — who had given her Mummy Two with genuine feeling but who had never, not entirely, placed himself in her hands the way Mirembe had.

Mirembe was hers. Mooli was his mother's. And that distinction, invisible at first, began to matter.

It began in small ways, as such things always begin.

A coolness that was not quite coldness. A patience that was slightly less patient than it had been. A way of including Mooli in decisions and conversations that had once been natural but now had the faint quality of effort — the effort visible underneath the naturalness, like a foundation crack that appears only in certain light. Mooli noticed. He noticed and he filed it away and he adjusted around it the way he had learned to adjust around difficult things: quietly, without announcement, without burdening anyone else with what he was observing.

He told himself it was nothing. He told himself he was imagining it, that he was looking for problems because part of him, the part that had loved and lost Natunga, was always waiting for the next loss, always scanning the horizon for the shape of the thing that would take something from him.

He told himself these things. He did not entirely believe them.

* * *

The word that changed everything was spoken on an ordinary evening.

Wanyakala was home for dinner — he had been making an effort, in these years, to be more consistently present, and he largely succeeded. They ate together as a family: the four of them around the table, the city outside doing what it always did, the compound quiet in the way it was quiet in the evenings. The meal was good. The conversation was the kind of conversation that families have — practical, circling, the exchange of the day's small events.

And then Mooli, who had been sitting quietly and eating and listening, said something to Mariana. Something about a dream he had had, something about his father telling him that Mariana was going to fall sick for three days. He said it the way he said things that came from the dream-space — carefully, as though he was not sure how they would be received, but unable not to say them because the not-saying felt worse than the saying.

The table went still.

Wanyakala looked at Mariana. Mariana looked at Mooli. And in the look that passed between them — in the very specific quality of her stillness, in the way her hand stopped moving and her face went through something rapid and barely controlled — Mooli understood that he had said something that reached further than he had intended.

He did not understand, then, what he had touched. He was too young, and the connection between his dream and the real world was something he had not yet learned to interpret with precision. He knew only that the evening had changed quality, that the room had taken on a tension that pressed against his skin, and that Mariana was looking at him in a way she had never looked at him before.

Not with warmth. Not with the performative warmth, not even with the cooler version that had replaced it in recent months. With something that had no warmth in it at all.

Two days later, Mariana fell sick.

Exactly as Mooli had said she would. The illness arrived on the morning of the second day after dinner — a sudden thing, significant enough to keep her in bed, significant enough to require the doctor and then, quietly, under circumstances that Wanyakala was not fully informed of, a visit to someone else entirely. Someone who dealt in older remedies. Someone who understood, in the framework that Mariana navigated between the modern world and older traditions, how to address an illness that felt like more than an illness.

She recovered on the third day.

Wanyakala was relieved. He attributed the illness to something passing — a bug, a fatigue, the ordinary vulnerabilities of the body. He did not connect it to the dinner table conversation. He was a practical man who organised the world through cause and effect that he could see, and the territory between a child's dream and a woman's illness was not territory he naturally entered.

Mariana connected it.

She lay in her room on the third day, recovered and not recovered, and she thought about the boy with the watchful eyes who had looked at her across the dinner table and told her, calmly and without drama, that she was going to fall sick. She thought about all the times she had caught him watching her — not with hostility, but with that particular quality of attention that sees more than it is supposed to see. She thought about the years of his quietness and his careful adjustment and his Mummy Two and the fact that he had never, not once, fully given her the thing she needed from him: complete surrender, complete acceptance, the uncomplicated love that Mirembe gave without reservation.

She thought about the company. About what Wanyakala had built and what it was worth. About the shares she had asked for and received. About the marriage certificate that placed her name alongside his in the documentation of a life that was, by any measure, enormously valuable.

She thought about what stood between her and the certainty of that life.

And somewhere in that thinking — in the place where fear and calculation and old buried things that should have stayed buried came together — a thought arrived that should have been dismissed immediately and was not.

* * *

Mooli did not know any of this.

He was a child. He knew that the dinner had changed something, that the quality of Mariana's gaze toward him had shifted in a way that made the back of his neck prickle. He knew that she had been sick and had recovered and that since her recovery the house felt different — a tension in it that was subtle but constant, like a low frequency sound that you cannot identify but that you feel in the chest.

He said nothing to his father. He had learned this silence over years — the silence of a child who loves his parent too much to add to their weight, who has observed that certain things, if spoken, produce more damage than the damage they were trying to prevent.

He watched. He waited. He kept to his own rooms and his own world and he was kind to Mirembe and respectful to Mariana and obedient in all the ways that Natunga had taught him obedience was a form of grace rather than a form of defeat.

And Mariana, watching him watch her, felt something that she would not have named aloud but that was, underneath all its other names, fear. The boy was not going to stop seeing her. He was not going to be managed or contained or gradually persuaded into blindness. He was going to keep looking, and one day he was going to see something specific enough to say aloud, and Wanyakala was going to hear it.

She needed the boy gone.

But first, she needed the other thing. The thing that would make everything else unnecessary, that would make security absolute and permanent and independent of what any watchful child said at any dinner table.

She began to speak to Wanyakala about the future.

Not obviously — she was not an obvious woman. She approached it obliquely, the way water approaches a wall: finding the small gaps, applying gentle pressure, returning to the same places over time until something gave. She spoke about providing for Mirembe. She spoke about what would happen to the family if — God forbid — something unexpected occurred. She spoke about the company and its management and the importance of clear documentation, of arrangements made in advance rather than in the chaos of emergency.

Wanyakala, who was a practical man and who loved his family and who had indeed been somewhat informal in his arrangements for the future, listened. He consulted his lawyers. He made adjustments. He extended to Mariana, in the language of property and shares and documentation, the full expression of his trust in her as his wife and the mother of his child.

She received it with gratitude that was completely convincing.

She then proposed that they go to church and make it official — not just the civil arrangements, but the church marriage, the public declaration, the ceremony before God and family that would make her status as his wife unambiguous and unassailable in every arena that mattered.

They went to church on a Saturday morning in the dry season, in a ceremony smaller than their first but no less formal, and they stood before the altar and made their vows again, and Mariana stood in white that suited her extraordinarily well and made the promises that she had decided to make, and Wanyakala stood beside her with the face of a man who believes in what he is doing and believed it completely.

Her family celebrated with a joy that was entirely genuine. They were women who loved Mariana and wanted her secured, and secured she now was, in every document and every arrangement and every certificate that the law and the church between them could produce.

The celebration went on into the evening.

Mooli sat through it with his careful face and his watchful eyes and said the appropriate things at the appropriate moments, and nobody, not even Wanyakala, could have said precisely what the boy was thinking.

* * *

The days that followed had a quality that Mooli would later struggle to describe — a held quality, like the air before a storm, when everything is ordinary and nothing is ordinary simultaneously. Wanyakala continued as he always had: working, coming home, present in the evenings, attentive to his children and his wife. The compound ran on its usual rhythms. Otim moved through his duties. Mirembe went to school and came back and demanded stories in the evenings and Mooli provided them, sitting beside her on the small bed with the lamp on and his voice making the shapes that her imagination required.

But underneath the ordinary rhythms, something was moving.

Mooli felt it the way he felt things — not through evidence he could name but through the body, through a persistent unease that lived in his chest and would not be reasoned away. He watched Mariana more carefully than before. He noticed when she was in the kitchen alone, what she was doing, how long she spent there. He noticed when Wanyakala was late and when he was not. He noticed the specific quality of her attention to his father — the way it had sharpened recently, become more focused, as though she was paying a particular kind of attention to him that was different from ordinary spousal attention.

He told himself he was imagining it.

He was not imagining it.

It was a Thursday evening. Wanyakala had called from the office to say he was running late — a common enough occurrence, not alarming, the ordinary friction of a business that required his presence in more places than the day could always accommodate. Mariana received the call and said the words that wives say and ended the call and stood for a moment in the kitchen with her phone in her hand.

Mooli was in his room. He heard nothing specific — no sound that would later constitute evidence, no detail that could be pointed to in retrospect as the moment of decision. He heard only the ordinary sounds of the household in the late afternoon, and then the kitchen, and then the smell of dinner beginning to be prepared.

He did not go down for dinner when it was ready.

He was not hungry. He had homework that he used as the reason, and it was not entirely untrue — there was homework — but the deeper truth was the unease that had been building for days, that had reached on this particular evening a pitch that made being in the kitchen with Mariana, at the table where food was being served, feel like something he needed to avoid without quite knowing why.

He stayed in his room. He did his homework. He listened to the house.

Wanyakala came home later than expected — later than the call had suggested, the kind of late that happens when the day accumulates more than it was supposed to. Mooli heard the gate, the car, the familiar sequence of sounds that meant his father was home. He heard the front door, the voices — Mariana welcoming him, warm and present, the specific register of a wife greeting a husband after a long day.

He heard his father moving through the house. Heard him pause — in the kitchen, probably, or the dining room. Heard the quieter sounds of a man who has come home very late and is very hungry and has found that there is food.

And then, not long after, Wanyakala came to Mooli's room.

He knocked lightly and came in when Mooli answered, and he sat on the edge of the bed in the way he sometimes sat when he wanted to talk but was not quite sure what he wanted to say. He looked tired. He looked the particular tiredness of a man who has been working since before the sun came up and has not stopped.

"You didn't eat," he said.

"I wasn't hungry," Mooli said.

Wanyakala looked at him for a moment.

"I haven't eaten either," he said. "I was going to have what Mariana left but —" He paused. Something crossed his face. Not pain, exactly, not yet. Something more preliminary than pain. "I don't think I'm hungry either. Long day."

He said goodnight. He went to his room.

Mooli sat in his bed with the lamp on and the homework finished and something very cold and very still settling into him — not panic, because panic is loud, and what he felt was quiet. A quietness that felt like clarity. A stillness in which a truth he had been trying not to think directly was making itself unavoidable.

He did not sleep well that night.

In the morning, Wanyakala did not come down to breakfast.

By midmorning, he was sick. By the afternoon, he was very sick. By the evening, the doctor had come and gone and come again, and the household had assumed the specific quality of a place where something serious is happening — hushed, focused, the ordinary business of the day suspended.

Mariana moved through it all with the efficiency of a woman managing a crisis. She called the family. She spoke to the doctor. She arranged and organised and communicated, and her face carried the appropriate expression of a wife frightened for her husband, and those who saw her would have said she was frightened and meant it.

Mooli stood in the corridor outside his father's room and watched her and felt the cold stillness from the night before deepen into something that had no name but that he recognised as the worst thing he had ever felt.

He knew.

He did not know how he knew. He could not have constructed a logical argument, could not have pointed to specific evidence, could not have explained in terms that an adult would have accepted as sufficient. But he knew with the certainty of someone for whom knowing and feeling have always occupied the same space — the certainty of a child who sees things, who has always seen things, who has learned to trust what he sees even when the adults around him are telling a different story.

He knew what had happened to his father.

And he knew who had done it.

Wanyakala died before the second morning.

In the language of the household, in the language of the family gathering that assembled rapidly and filled the compound and the sitting room with grief and noise and the specific pandemonium of unexpected death — in all of that language, it was described as poison. Not the poison Mooli knew about. Something else. The workers at the company, Mariana told them, her voice steady and her eyes wet — there had been enemies, there had been business rivals, there had been people who stood to gain from Wanyakala's absence. She said this with conviction and detail and the kind of specificity that makes a story credible, and the family received it because they needed something to receive, because grief requires a story and the story Mariana offered had the shape of one that could be believed.

The house filled and then emptied and then filled again. Relatives came from places Mooli had not known his father had relatives. Wanyakala's business associates arrived in cars and with condolences. The church sent someone. The neighbours came. The compound, which had always been a place of controlled activity, became for those days a place of controlled chaos — people moving through it in the rhythms of mourning, the cooking and the prayer and the sitting with and the speaking of the dead in the past tense that is the hardest grammatical shift a person ever has to make.

Mooli moved through all of it.

He was eight years old — old enough to understand everything that was happening and not old enough to do anything about it. He sat through the prayers and the meals and the long evenings of adult conversation about a man he had loved and was now being asked to mourn in public in addition to private. He held Mirembe's hand when she cried, and she cried often, in the abandoned way of a young child who does not yet know that grief is supposed to be managed.

He did not cry in public. He was his father's son in this, among other things.

He watched Mariana.

He watched her be a widow. He watched her perform it — not because everything she expressed was false, because he did not know that, but because he was watching with the specific attention of someone who is looking for the places where the performance and the reality diverge. He watched and he saw things he could not name but could not unfeel.

The burial happened on a cold morning with the city visible from the hill where they stood. Wanyakala went into the ground with his name and his dates and the people who had loved him standing in the particular formation that mourners make, and Mooli stood among them and watched and felt the full weight of what it means to lose a parent for the second time before the age of ten.

He was eight years old.

And the world had shown him its other face again.

Chapter Six: Grace to Grass

After the burial, the house changed.

It changed in the way that houses change when the person who held them together is gone — not all at once, not dramatically, but through the gradual withdrawal of whatever it was that person provided. Wanyakala had provided many things: income, presence, the particular quality of authority that a responsible man brings to a household, the sense that decisions were being made and the future was being tended. Without him, the house did not collapse immediately. It simply began, slowly and then less slowly, to become a different kind of place.

Mariana owned it now. Owned it legally, completely, in every document that the lawyers had prepared in those careful months before Wanyakala died. She owned the house on the hill and she owned the shares and she owned the company — not in the way that a custodian owns something, not in trust for the children whose father had built it, but in her own name, for her own purposes, with the full backing of a legal arrangement that had been assembled with great care.

She moved through the house differently after the burial.

Not immediately — she was careful, and the weeks following a death are watched weeks, when the world is paying attention and expecting performance of grief, and she performed it well. But Mooli, who had been watching her for years now, who had developed an eye for the difference between the performed and the real, began to notice the changes accumulating.

A sharpness in the voice that had not been there before. A quickness to displeasure. A quality of impatience with the small claims of daily life that suggested her attention was elsewhere, occupied with larger calculations than the running of a household and the raising of a child.

He noticed. He adjusted. He made himself smaller, quieter, less present in the parts of the house where she spent most of her time. He organised his life around her schedule — not out of love now, but out of the preservation instinct that children develop when love is no longer the operative principle in a household.

Gyabi came to the house three weeks after the burial.

He was Wanyakala's uncle — a man of the old kind, deeply rooted in the traditions that Wanyakala's generation had grown up with and mostly left behind without entirely discarding. He was old in the way of men who have lived hard and deliberately, with a face that had accumulated its years visibly and eyes that held the particular quality of someone who has learned to see in multiple registers simultaneously. He carried himself with the gravity of a man who understands that certain things require him to be present whether or not he has been invited.

He arrived without announcement. He sat in the sitting room and refused tea and looked around the house with those layered eyes, and the looking was not casual.

He had been to his room. The secret room — the room in the back of his compound where he kept the things that belonged to the older world, the instruments and materials of a knowledge that predated the city and the lawyers and the documentation that now governed who owned what. He had gone there after the burial, in the days when the loss was still raw and the questions it raised were still loud in him, and what he had encountered there he described to no one initially.

But he had seen. He had been shown — in whatever way such things are shown to men like him — the face of what had happened to his nephew. He had been given understanding, through channels that could not be explained in the language of the city but that were nonetheless real to him and reliable, that Wanyakala had not died of a business rival's malice.

He organised a family meeting.

It was held in the compound of the oldest uncle — a neutral space, chosen deliberately, where no single person's ownership of the gathering could be assumed. Wanyakala's relatives came in numbers: brothers and sisters and cousins and the aunts who had raised him and who now sat with their grief arranged in the formal postures of women who are managing something larger than personal loss. Mariana came too, because she was his wife — his widow — and the meeting was nominally about the family's plans going forward, about the company and the property and the arrangements for the children.

Gyabi spoke when the time came.

He spoke in the careful language of a man who knows that what he is saying cannot be proven in any court and who is saying it anyway, because some truths exist in a jurisdiction that courts cannot reach. He spoke about what he had seen. About what had been shown to him. About the specific details of his vision — the face, the circumstances, the sequence of events in the kitchen on the Thursday evening — that corresponded with things the family could verify independently if they chose to.

The room was very quiet while he spoke.

Mariana sat in it with a stillness that was different from the stillness of grief. A controlled stillness. A stillness that was also a calculation.

When Gyabi finished, the family turned toward her.

She was ready for them.

She had lawyers. She had documents. She had the church certificate and the property registration and the bank accounts and the company shares and every piece of paper that the modern world produces to establish who a person is and what belongs to them. She had the legal architecture that she and Wanyakala had assembled in those careful months, and it was sound in every way that mattered to the systems she had navigated, and the systems she had navigated were the systems that governed what could be done about what Gyabi was saying.

She went to court.

The case was heard by a judge who dealt in evidence and documentation and the kind of proof that can be filed and referenced and examined by professionals. Gyabi's vision was not that kind of proof. The family's suspicions, however deep and however well-founded in their own framework of understanding, were not that kind of proof. The death had been attributed to poisoning, yes — the medical evidence was not in dispute — but the attribution of that poisoning to a specific person required a standard of evidence that the family did not have and could not produce.

Mariana won.

She stood outside the court building on the day of the judgment with her lawyers beside her and the city going on around her and her name confirmed, legally and completely, as the owner of everything Wanyakala had built. She did not celebrate publicly. She was too careful for that, and too aware of the family watching from across the distance that the judgment had just formalised.

The enmity between Mariana and Wanyakala's family became, from that day, a permanent feature of the landscape. The uncles and the aunts and the cousins who had gathered at the burial and had hoped for a different outcome now reorganised themselves around their loss — not just the loss of Wanyakala, but the loss of everything he had built, which had been folded into his widow's name and placed beyond their reach.

They could not touch the property. They could not touch the company.

But they had not stopped watching.

* * *

Mooli was nine years old when he understood, clearly and completely and without any remaining doubt, what had happened to his father.

He had been moving through the months after the burial in the way that he had learned to move through difficult things: quietly, with attention, making himself useful where possible and invisible where necessary. He went to school. He came home. He looked after Mirembe with the particular tenderness that had always characterised his relationship with his sister — unchanged and unchanging, the one thing in his life that felt entirely clean.

He had the dream on a Friday night.

He had been dreaming of his father since the burial — not frightening dreams, not the kind that leave you gasping, but the gentle and coherent dreams of grief, where the dead appear and speak and behave as though the boundary between living and gone is more permeable than waking life suggests. In these dreams, Wanyakala was as Mooli remembered him: measured, present, with that specific calm that had always made Mooli feel that everything was manageable.

In this dream, Wanyakala sat across from him in a room that was not any room Mooli had been in but that felt familiar in the way of dream-spaces, which create familiarity from nothing. He looked at his son directly and said what he said clearly and without softening, because the dead, in Mooli's experience of them, did not soften things. They said what needed saying.

He told Mooli what had happened. Not the full complexity of it — not the calculation and the documentation and the legal architecture — but the essential truth of it, the thing that Mooli had already half-known and had not allowed himself to know fully.

He told him to be careful.

Mooli woke before the sun came up. He lay in the dark of his room and he held the dream carefully, the way you hold something you do not want to break, and he knew that the time of adjusting and making himself small was ending. The dream had said what it said with the clarity of a truth that has been withheld long enough and is no longer willing to be withheld.

That evening, at dinner, the food was on the table when Mooli came down. He sat. Mariana sat. Mirembe sat and talked about her day in the way that young children talk about their days — comprehensively, without selection, including everything.

Mooli looked at the food.

He ate very little that evening. He said he was tired. He went to bed early. And whatever intuition or vision or simple accumulated intelligence was operating in him that night — whatever it was — it kept him from eating more than the few spoonfuls he managed, pushed around the plate, not quite enough to matter.

In the morning, there was a commotion.

Mariana was sick.

Not as sick as Wanyakala had been. Not dying-sick. But sick enough, and sick in a way that produced in Mooli, standing in the corridor outside her room listening to the household respond to the emergency, a feeling he did not know what to do with. A feeling that was not quite relief and not quite fear and not quite grief but contained elements of all three, arranged in a way that had no name.

He went to school. He came back. The doctor had come and gone.

She recovered.

And in the days of her recovery, something shifted in the house that was beyond anything Mooli had encountered before. The coolness that had been building for months — the narrowed gaze, the impatience, the quality of a woman whose calculations were no longer requiring her to perform warmth — dropped away entirely. What was underneath it was not warmth either. What was underneath it was something colder and more final.

She did not look at him the way she used to. She did not look at him the way she had looked at him in the bad months after the burial. She looked at him the way you look at something you have decided to remove.

Mooli felt it and he did not know what to do with the feeling and he carried it for the days that followed in the private, costly way that he had always carried the things that had no one to receive them.

* * *

It happened on a Tuesday after school.

He came through the compound gate in the late afternoon, his school bag on his back, the city noise settling into its late-afternoon register. The compound was quiet. Otim was not visible. The house had a quality that he recognised in retrospect but did not name in the moment — the quality of a space that has been arranged, prepared, set up for something.

Mariana called him inside.

Her voice was ordinary. Not warm, but not hostile — the careful neutrality of a woman who is managing her expression. She told him there was a message from someone, that a driver needed to take him somewhere, that it was fine, that it was taken care of. She said these things with a matter-of-factness that Mooli, who had been reading her for years, could not quite locate the alarm in, because the alarm was not visible. It was underneath the matter-of-factness. But he was nine years old and she was the adult and the compound was quiet and Otim was nowhere.

The driver was a man Mooli had not seen before.

He held the car door open and indicated for Mooli to get in and Mooli hesitated — for a long moment, for a moment that he would replay in the years ahead, standing at the car door with his school bag still on his back and the compound quiet around him and the late afternoon light coming slanted across the bougainvillea on the compound wall. He hesitated and looked back at the house and saw Mariana at the window, watching, with an expression that was not readable from this distance but that communicated, even at this distance, something that settled the decision.

He got in the car.

They drove.

He did not know how long they drove. He was not certain, at first, that anything was wrong — children accommodate uncertainty more readily than adults because uncertainty is a larger proportion of their ordinary experience. He watched the city go past the window. He watched it change from the neighbourhood he knew to neighbourhoods he knew less well to neighbourhoods he did not know at all, and then to the edges of the city, and then to roads that were not the roads of Kampala but of somewhere further.

He asked where they were going.

The driver said nothing.

He asked again.

The driver pulled the car over. He reached into the back without speaking and he put a cloth over Mooli's eyes and tied it, not roughly but firmly, with the efficiency of someone who has been instructed and is following instructions. Mooli's heart was loud in his ears. He did not cry out — there was no one to cry out to, and some part of him understood already that crying out was not the appropriate tool for this situation. He sat in the back of the car with his eyes covered and his school bag in his lap and his hands gripping the straps, and the car drove for what felt like a very long time.

When it stopped, the driver opened the door.

He removed the cloth from Mooli's eyes.

Around him was a part of Kampala he did not recognise — not the ordered, paved streets of the hill where the house was, not the organised commercial areas he knew from school trips and weekend errands, but a different city. The city beneath the city, the one that exists in the gaps between the planned places, where the buildings lean and the streets narrow and the smell is layered in ways that speak of a thousand lives being lived very close together with very little between them.

The driver looked at him.

"You don't go back," he said. His voice was not cruel. It was the voice of someone delivering an instruction that they have been paid to deliver and are not responsible for. "You understand? You don't go back."

He got back in the car. He drove away.

Mooli stood on the street with his school bag on his back and the afternoon light going gold around him and the city moving in its indifferent way and understood, with the clean and devastating clarity of a nine-year-old who has been paying attention his entire life, that he was alone.

Not temporarily. Not in the way of being left at a gate while someone parks. Alone in the absolute sense. Without a house to go back to. Without a father to call. Without a mother, either mother, in any form.

He stood on the street for a long time.

Then he sat down on the ground with his back against the wall and his school bag in his lap, and for the first time since the burial — for the first time since he had decided at eight years old to manage his grief quietly and not burden anyone with it — he cried.

He cried for his father. He cried for Natunga, who had died before he was old enough to cry for her properly. He cried for Mirembe, who was at home in the house on the hill with a woman who had just had her brother driven to the edge of the city and left there, and who would be told something plausible and would believe it because she was too young to know better. He cried for the specific quality of the afternoon light that reminded him, in some way he could not name, of Saturday afternoons in the compound when everything had been alright.

He cried until there was nothing left to cry.

Then he stood up.

He wiped his face with the back of his hand. He adjusted the straps of his school bag. He looked at the street around him — the people moving through it, the sellers and the walkers and the children who belonged to this part of the city in ways he did not yet understand but would learn.

He was nine years old. He had survived two deaths and one exile and the long cold withdrawal of a woman who had decided he was inconvenient. He was standing on a street in a part of Kampala he did not know with a school bag full of books and no money and no address.

He began to walk.

He did not know where he was walking to. He walked because standing still felt like surrender, and whatever he was — whatever the combination of Wanyakala's discipline and Natunga's love and his own watchful nature had made of him — he was not someone who surrendered.

The city closed around him.

He walked into it.

* * *

The streets of Kampala are not kind to children alone on them.

This is not a special unkindness — it is the ordinary indifference of any urban space to those who do not have a place in it, who have not yet found the specific role or the specific community that transforms a city from a threatening environment into a navigable one. The city is not cruel. It simply does not accommodate what it has not been asked to accommodate, and Mooli, walking through it in his school uniform with his school bag and his grief, had not been asked for.

He slept the first night under the awning of a shop that was closed, curled against the wall with his bag under his head, listening to the city do what cities do at night — louder in some ways, quieter in others, but never actually still. He was cold in a way that surprised him. Kampala is not a cold city in the way of highland places, but the nights have a chill that the days conceal, and he had not brought a jacket because he had not known he was going to need one.

He did not sleep much.

In the morning, he watched how things worked. He had always been someone who watched before participating, who understood that observation is the necessary precondition of effective action. He watched the people who lived on these streets — how they moved, how they organised themselves, where they gathered, how they ate. He watched the children specifically, because he was a child and he understood that the relevant information for his situation was the information held by those who were in similar situations.

There were others. Of course there were others. Kampala, like every large city in the world, has its population of children without houses, who have ended up on the streets through one form of loss or another — death, displacement, abandonment, the various ways that the adult world fails the young — and who have learned, in the specific hard school of the street, how to survive in a place that was not designed for them.

They found him, or he found them. It happened organically, in the way that people in extremity find each other — through proximity and mutual recognition, through the shared grammar of a situation that only those inside it can fully read.

He learned.

He learned which areas of the city were navigable and which were not, which shopkeepers would give you something left over at the end of the day if you asked in the right way and which would chase you, which places were safe to sleep and which were not. He learned the traffic patterns of the city — the hours when the roads filled and the hours when they thinned — because the traffic was also an economy, a place where money moved between windows and outstretched hands if you moved through it at the right moment with the right approach.

He discovered, in those first weeks on the streets, something about himself that surprised him: he was not without resources. Not material resources — he had almost none of those — but interior resources. The watchfulness that had always defined him was, in this environment, a genuine advantage. He read situations quickly and accurately. He made decisions from good information rather than from panic or wishful thinking. He was not reckless in the way of children who have not learned yet what the consequences of recklessness look like, because he had been learning about consequences since he was four years old and had been paying attention the whole time.

He survived the first month. And the second.

And through those months, and through the years that followed — thirteen years in total, years that took him from nine years old to twenty-two, from a boy in a school uniform on an unknown street to a young man who knew every corner of the city's underside — through all of it, the dreams continued.

His mother came to him in sleep.

Not every night. Not on demand. But regularly enough, and consistently enough, and with sufficient clarity that he could not dismiss them as the ordinary workings of a grieving mind. Natunga appeared in the way she had always appeared to him in the years when she was alive — present, specific, concerned with the practical details of his situation rather than with any larger metaphysical statement. She told him things. She showed him places, directed him away from situations that, when he woke and moved through the day, proved to be as she had indicated — dangerous, or wrong, or worth avoiding for reasons that became clear only afterward.

She told him he was not forgotten.

Wanyakala came too, in the dreams, less frequently but no less clearly. He came as he had been before the end — measured, calm, with the specific quality of presence that had always made his son feel that things could be managed. He did not explain the events of the kitchen on the Thursday evening. He did not revisit what Mariana had done. He spoke, when he spoke, of the future — of things coming, of a time when the streets would be behind Mooli, of people who were being arranged, though Wanyakala did not use that word, into a configuration that would eventually be meaningful.

Mooli received these dreams the way he received difficult things: carefully, without forcing them into shape, holding them lightly enough that they could continue to mean what they meant rather than what he needed them to mean.

He grew.

On the streets of Kampala, over thirteen years, Mooli grew from a nine-year-old boy in a school uniform into a young man of twenty-two who carried himself with a particular quality that people noticed without always being able to name. He was not hardened in the way that the streets can harden people — or rather, he was hardened in some respects and remained unexpectedly soft in others, the combination producing someone who was both more capable and more compassionate than the circumstances of his formation might have suggested.

He found his own ways of being useful in the city. He learned the traffic economy thoroughly and worked it well, moving through the jams with an efficiency that came from years of observation and a fundamental understanding of human generosity — when it was available, how to approach it, how not to press it past the point where it collapsed into irritation. He was known, over time, in his section of the city — not famous, not distinguished, but known in the way that consistent and reliable people become known in any community, as someone whose character could be depended upon.

He did not stop thinking about school.

This was perhaps the most persistent thing about him — the refusal, through all thirteen years, to let go of the idea of education, of the life that had been interrupted at Primary One when the house on the hill was still a home and his father was still alive. He carried it the way you carry a debt you intend to repay: with full awareness, with no intention of forgetting, with the patient certainty of someone who has decided that the postponement is temporary even when the evidence suggests otherwise.

He coached himself where he could. There were people on the streets who had more education than their circumstances currently reflected — teachers brought low by one misfortune or another, clerks and administrators whose situations had changed in the ways that situations change in a city that is also subject to the ordinary turbulence of a country finding itself. He found these people. He attached himself to them in the ways that he had learned to attach himself to useful things — not parasitically, but through the exchange of what he had for what they had, which was sometimes simply his company and his attention and the quality of his listening.

He learned. Slowly, partially, in the gaps between the business of surviving, he continued to learn.

And the dreams continued.

And somewhere in the city, in the directions the dreams sometimes indicated and sometimes simply implied, the configuration that Wanyakala had spoken of was assembling itself, piece by piece, with the patience of things that are going to happen regardless and are simply waiting for the right moment.

Mooli did not know what that moment would look like.

He knew only that he would recognise it when it came.

He had been paying attention his entire life. He was not going to stop now.
